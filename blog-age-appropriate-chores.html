Age-appropriate chores: a practical guide from toddlers to teens

A realistic list of chores children can grow into, plus tips for introducing responsibility without overwhelm.

By The Chores team · Updated August 8, 2026

In this guide

  1. Ages 2–3
  2. Ages 4–5
  3. Ages 6–8
  4. Ages 9–12
  5. Ages 13+
  6. Introducing a chore

The best chore for a child is not the one another family assigns at the same age. It is the one your child can learn safely, repeat with growing confidence, and eventually complete with less help.

Use age ranges as a starting point—not a test. Ability, attention, motor skills, family culture, and the complexity of your home all matter more than a birthday.

Ages 2–3: helping alongside you

Toddlers are learning the rhythm of helping. Keep tasks tiny, visible, and done together. Success means participating, not producing a perfect result.

  • Put toys into a low basket
  • Carry napkins to the table
  • Place clothes in a hamper
  • Wipe a small spill with help

Ages 4–5: short jobs with a clear finish

Preschoolers do well when a task has one or two steps and an obvious ending. A picture cue can work better than repeated verbal instructions.

  • Make the bed by pulling up the comforter
  • Feed a pet with a pre-measured scoop
  • Clear non-breakable dishes
  • Water a small plant

Ages 6–8: building a dependable routine

Early elementary kids can begin owning recurring tasks. Teach the job once, practice together, then let the routine—not constant reminders—carry more of the load.

  • Load or unload safe dishwasher items
  • Fold towels and simple clothing
  • Sweep a small area
  • Put away their own clean laundry

Ages 9–12: owning a whole outcome

Tweens can handle connected steps and begin noticing what a finished job looks like. This is a good time to distinguish daily family contributions from extra paid work.

  • Change bed sheets
  • Clean a bathroom sink and mirror
  • Prepare a simple snack
  • Manage their weekly laundry

Ages 13+: contributing like a capable household member

Teen chores can teach life skills they will need outside your home. Rotate responsibilities so they practice cooking, cleaning, planning, and basic home care.

  • Plan and cook a family meal
  • Deep-clean a bathroom
  • Make a grocery list and shop with a budget
  • Handle laundry from sorting through putting away

How to introduce a new chore

  • Show the finished result first
  • Do it together while narrating only the important steps
  • Let your child try while you stay nearby
  • Use one neutral reminder instead of repeated corrections
  • Notice effort and consistency before speed

The takeaway

Start smaller than you think, define what “done” means, and add responsibility only after the current routine feels ordinary.
